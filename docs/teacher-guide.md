# Still Out There: a 40-minute lesson

**Ages:** 9–14 · **Subjects:** science, geography, maths · **Languages:** English, French, Arabic (switch at the top of the page)
**You need:** the website on any phone, tablet or computer with a browser. Nothing to install, no account, free.

## The big idea

People have left machines on the Moon, on Mars and in deep space. They stopped talking, but they never left. Each one was sent to answer a question. This lesson asks pupils to find out what each machine discovered and where it is today.

## Plan

| Time | What pupils do | Where on the site |
|---|---|---|
| 5 min | **Hook.** Show the Moon. Ask: *"Is anything of ours up there right now? Is it day or night where it sits?"* Let pupils guess, then read the answer on screen. | Moon page, "Right now" |
| 5 min | **Roll call.** Play it for the whole class. Ten machines answer in the order they went quiet. The last one says: *"Still working."* Ask: *"Which one, and how can a machine on the Moon still work?"* | Roll call |
| 15 min | **Detectives.** In pairs, each pair picks one machine and reads (or listens to) its five chapters, then fills in the four boxes below. | A machine → "Hear my story" |
| 10 min | **Can you save Opportunity?** Play together, voting on each choice. Nothing works, because nothing worked in real life either. Talk about it: *"Was the mission a failure?"* (It was designed to last 90 Martian days. The mission ran from 2004 to 2019.) | Mars → "Can you save Opportunity?" |
| 5 min | **Find it from orbit.** At the end of the game, pupils search a real photo taken from 268 km above Mars for one tiny dot: Opportunity. | Game ending, or Opportunity's page |

## Detective sheet (one per pair)

1. **Why was my machine sent?** What question did it try to answer?
2. **What did it find?** One discovery, in your own words.
3. **Why did it go quiet?** Dust, cold, a broken part, or the mission simply ended?
4. **Where is it now?** Is it day or night there right now? (The page shows it live.)

## Extensions

- **Maths: light is fast but not instant.** On the laser retroreflector's page, fire the laser. Light travels about 300,000 km every second and the Moon is about 384,000 km away. Ask pupils to work out why the round trip takes about 2.5 seconds.
- **Maths: big numbers.** On the Deep space page, Pioneer 10 is more than 20 billion km away. How long would a radio message take to get there? (The page shows the answer live.)
- **Science: dust.** On InSight's page, compare the 2024 photo from orbit with the caption: the lander used to look blue from space and is now red-brown. Why does dust settle on it?
- **Myth or fact?** Three famous space stories. Pupils vote, then reveal the sourced answer.

## Why you can trust it

Every fact on the site links to a NASA source you can open (the "Real data" drawers and the Sources page). Where NASA has not published a number, the site leaves it out rather than guessing.
